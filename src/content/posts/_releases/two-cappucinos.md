## Two Cappuccinos

"*Due cappuccini. Mille grazie.*" I follow behind with her helmet tucked
underarm as Alessia balances our coffees to a table overlooking the
Tiber. In the distance, a group of middle-aged Americans are getting
their photo in front of *Castel Sant'Angelo*. It's Monday after Easter -
*Pasquetta* as they call it - a public holiday for them but another full
day of sightseeing for me.

"It's a good habit." I've put her on my insane schedule and she lets me
know it. "Getting up early. I need to study," She tells me,
absentmindedly stirring the foam before she sips. Italians have such a
peculiar way of doing things.

We met six months prior in the remote mountains of northern Vietnam. The
Ha Giang loop draws riders of every stripe. Young Israelis celebrating
the end of their national service, German students on sabbatical, a
British couple or two, and the occasional clueless American. Present.

During the day, you navigate mountain roads in either blistering heat or
torrential rain. My prior motorcycle experience consisted of three hours
in a Harley Davidson parking lot. By the time the third old lady on a
scooter passes me, pride dictates falling off the mountain would be
preferable to being overtaken by a fourth.

At night, you pray there's room at the guest house in whichever town you
stumble across. Any respite from the hours of engine drone and unending
rice terraces. Dinner is served family style. Everyone gets a rice bowl
and an unlimited supply of happy water - fermented corn wine. Strong
fermented corn wine. Stories from the road are exchanged.

The Israelis inevitably fall back into indecipherable Hebrew. The German
leaves for an early bedtime. The British couple go off to do what
couples do. And I'm left sitting across a lady with a cigarette.
Italians have such a peculiar way of doing things.

Alessia's a medical student. My American brain tried to categorize her.
Either she was so brilliant she could take time off, or so terrible they
let her. Her riding stories make mine sound trivial. She's been passing
those old ladies on dodgy scooters. We talk late into the night. Kindred
spirits find each other. Even in rural Vietnam.

Back to Rome. "Did you know they used to fill this place with water and
have ship battles?" I look around Piazza Navona. "How?" She shrugs.
"It's something they told us in school." An indictment of the Italian
public school system. We walk down more cobblestone streets. "I would
like to road trip in America one day, but my boyfriend is in the
military and he's not adventurous. He doesn't care to go anywhere."

"You should definitely do it while you're still young. Before children."
The words fly out of my mouth. I was a new uncle. I would not meet my
niece for another year. Polite convention be damned. "I cannot imagine
wanting to bring children into this failing world," she says softly.
I've hit a nerve for both of us.

"You want to shape the world for the better. This is one way to do it."
I'm playing conversational jazz and praying the spirit backs me up.
"None of my friends want to have children. But I'm not sure." A moment
of tension. "I can see you've given it much thought. Not enough people
do that." My attempt at diplomacy.

We pass white marble. I sense another opening. "Is it true that Italians
no longer go?" I prod. "The last time I went was for a cousin's
communion. Maybe ten years ago." We stop to look at the faded angels and
unrecognizable saints. "I like the smaller ones. Fewer tourists." She
nodded like that made sense.

"You know what that stands for?" She gestures to a manhole cover with
the inscription S.P.Q.R. It's the first time I've noticed the letters.
"*Sono pazzi questi Romani.* All these Romans are crazy." I'm looking at
a future doctor.

We arrive at the steps of the Pantheon. "Tell me when you make it to
America." I hand her the helmet. "Hopefully one day. There's so much to
plan." I open for a hug. Alessia leans in. The sound of a kiss by my
ear. Italians have such a peculiar way of doing things.
